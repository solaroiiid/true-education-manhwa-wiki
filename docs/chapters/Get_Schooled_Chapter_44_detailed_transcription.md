## Strip 005 — Bathroom pictograms

A child raises an issue concerning men’s and women’s bathroom signs.

The discussion concerns whether the standard symbols—trousers for men and skirts or red coloring for women—are stereotypical or difficult to distinguish.

Some students say the familiar signs are useful and easy to recognize.

## Strip 006 — Na protects the first disagreement

When one child begins challenging another, Na tells the class not to cut the speaker off.

He explains that pictograms exist to communicate quickly and that scholars and designers can debate how much traditional symbolism is appropriate.

Most importantly, he says the children must be allowed to speak freely rather than immediately classify one answer as morally bad.

Yang outwardly agrees.

## Strip 007 — Na-young’s discomfort

Yang asks a girl—identified in the dialogue as Na-young or by a similar name—for her opinion.

The girl, with hair arranged in buns, says that the skirt and red coloring on women’s signs make her uncomfortable and that she dislikes red.

Yang praises the contribution.

## Strip 008 — Mockery begins

Other children respond harshly.

They say anyone who cannot distinguish the signs is stupid or cannot read.

One child repeats the language of earlier lessons, accusing the dissenting child or her opponents of being “discriminators.”

Insults such as “maggot” appear.

The debate quickly stops being about design.

## Strip 009 — Yang’s weak correction

Yang lightly tells the students not to compare or insult one another, but she also praises the class for becoming more active.

> “THE CLASS IS MORE LIVELY.”

She decides that she should use this style of discussion often.

Na watches carefully. He notices that Yang values visible participation even when the participation is becoming collective aggression.

## Strip 010 — Constructing a majority

Yang asks the class to raise their hands if they agree with Na-young.

Hands rise.

## Strip 011 — A second dissenting opinion

Another girl, wearing an orange or warm-colored shirt, says the argument feels too sensitive or exaggerated.

## Strip 012 — Social punishment

Students attack the girl’s identity rather than her point.

They tell her to use the men’s room, accuse her of betraying girls and the future, and say discrimination continues because of people like her.

The language escalates to:

> “JUST DIE.”

Other children tell her not to breathe or eat.

The phrases resemble the teacher’s moral categories but have become weapons in a peer hierarchy.

---

## Strip 013 — Yang permits escalation

Yang says one of the students is going too far, but she does not stop the structure producing the hostility.

She continues treating the exchange as educational debate.

The children learn that abuse is tolerated when framed as passionate opposition to discrimination.

---

## Strip 014 — A student leader mobilizes the room

The bun-haired girl or another socially dominant student rises and shouts:

> “EVERYBODY GET UP!”

Several children obey immediately.

The command reveals that the classroom has already developed factions and leaders beneath Yang’s claimed free discussion.

## Strip 015 — Physical attack

One child rushes another and kicks or strikes her.

Desks scrape, bodies collide, and the room erupts.

Students scream insults including:

> “KILL YOURSELF!”

> “DIE YOU BUGS!”

> “CRAZY BITCH!”

The language is extremely violent for elementary pupils and reflects the moral absolutism they have been taught to use against enemies.

## Strip 016 — The class divides

Two groups form across the classroom.

Children pull hair, kick, shove, and swarm around desks.

The debate over symbols has become a literal faction fight.

## Strip 017 — Na stops the fight

Na rises and slams or strikes a desk with enough force to create a large impact SFX.

## Strip 018 — The question Yang avoided

Na turns toward Yang.

He asks whether, whenever people disagree:

> “YOU BITE EACH OTHER AND MAKE ENEMIES!”

The children stare, breathing hard and disheveled.

## Strip 019 — Division as an educational outcome

Na continues:

> “IS THE CLASS BEING DIVIDED IN HALF…”

> “PART OF YOUR EDUCATIONAL PURPOSES?”

The question reframes the fight not as unpredictable child behavior but as the result of a classroom design that rewards moral labeling and visible factional loyalty.

# Chapter-level observations

### 1. The online forum closes Yang’s feedback loop

Instead of examining the children’s harm, Yang seeks an anonymous community that frames every criticism as an attack on teachers. Validation replaces reflection.

### 2. “Free discussion” is staged

Yang chooses the topic, creates a public vote, and rewards the language she recognizes as morally correct. The children are free only to intensify the framework she supplied.

### 3. Moral categories become gang identities

Once classmates are divided into people “for” and “against” discrimination, ordinary disagreement is treated as evidence that the other child is evil. The resulting violence mirrors the gang conflicts from earlier arcs.

### 4. Na judges education by its result

His final question avoids an abstract ideological debate. Whatever Yang intended, the measurable outcome is a class split into enemies and physically attacking itself.
