## Strip 004 — Changsung’s childhood environment

Jun-bin explains Changsung’s background.

His father’s business failed when Changsung was in approximately fourth grade.

Within a year, his mother left.

For nearly two years he lived alone with an alcoholic father.

During sixth grade, the father attempted a murder-suicide.

Holding a knife, he said:

> **LET’S DIE, CHANGSUNG.**

> **DAD WILL FOLLOW SOON.**

Changsung escaped the house in his pajamas.

Runaway youths taught him how to survive through theft, intimidation, and organized crime.

Jun-bin says the environment did not erase Changsung’s responsibility, but it poisoned every path available to him.

## Strip 005 — Adam’s House gave him an alternative identity

At Adam’s House, Changsung received regular food, a bed, predictable rules, and adults who returned after conflict rather than abandoning him.

The change was gradual.

He became capable of helping younger residents and imagining an adult future outside crime.

Jun-bin says he observed him closely for six months.

> **HE ISN’T THE KIND OF PERSON WHO WOULD ATTACK HIS VICTIM AGAIN.**

Na warns that belief can also become prejudice.

Collective experience and statistics exist because many apparently reformed offenders do relapse.

The investigation must respect Changsung’s possibility of change without pretending the past is irrelevant.

## Strip 007 — Day nine: the accusation collapses

After nine days of investigation, the TRPA finds surveillance and witness evidence that contradicts the accusation.

The alleged victim was attacked later in an alley by older youths.

Changsung was not the assailant.

The victim used those injuries to falsely accuse him, hoping to send him back to detention as revenge for the original crime.

News reports announce that the allegation was false and Changsung has been cleared.

Jun-bin and the facility staff welcome him back.

## Strip 008 — Exoneration does not erase suspicion

Changsung leaves custody.

## Strip 009 — The cigarette

Outside, Changsung lights a cigarette.

Jun-bin’s internal narration says:

> **I’VE ALWAYS BELIEVED IN YOU.**

The cigarette is not proof of violent relapse.

It does show that rehabilitation is incomplete, uneven, and vulnerable.

The young man cleared of assault still carries habits, secrets, and private behavior the adults around him may not fully know.

The chapter ends with both Jun-bin’s faith and the reader’s uncertainty intact.

# Chapter-level continuity notes

## Jun-bin’s reform began through a victim’s father

Park Kyungpil combined accountability with continued care, refusing both indulgence and permanent rejection.

## Changsung’s family history explains vulnerability without excusing crime

The chapter asks what choices remain to a child fleeing attempted murder by a parent.

## Reputation nearly substitutes for evidence

The victim’s accusation becomes credible because Changsung’s past makes every denial sound manipulative.

## The TRPA’s investigation protects rehabilitation itself

Without verification, a false charge would confirm the public belief that change is impossible.

## Exoneration is not a complete happy ending

Jun-bin’s trust survives, but the final cigarette and later chapters suggest Changsung’s life may contain more than the cleared accusation.
