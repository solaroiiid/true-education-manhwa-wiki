# Chapter-level continuity notes

## Hojun’s narration establishes the social setting

The rural district’s young Korean population has largely left, while immigrant farm workers and their children have become a demographic majority. The chapter does not portray diversity itself as the problem. The conflict comes from Mukhyn turning genuine social discrimination into permission to dominate a smaller group of Korean classmates.

## Mukhyn switches identities depending on what gives him power

When bullying Hojun, he describes “you Korean people” as a hostile outside group. When a teacher says “Korean students,” he insists that he is Korean too and invokes military service. The inconsistency is intentional: he treats racial and national identity as tools for avoiding accountability.

## Daniel refuses both passivity and conventional corporal punishment

Daniel calls himself nonviolent, yet he is not physically helpless. He catches Mukhyn’s punch effortlessly, withstands his strength, and handcuffs himself to him. The technique is coercive but not framed as a beating. Daniel intends to remove Mukhyn’s ability to flee, posture before classmates, or switch between victim and aggressor while forcing prolonged dialogue.

## Daniel’s own history explains his reaction

His account of anti-Asian racism in East Harlem allows him to recognize the difference between being discriminated against and weaponizing discrimination. His censored slur is deliberately provocative and ethically troubling, but its narrative purpose is to make Mukhyn experience the exact rage he routinely dismisses in Korean victims.

## The chapter’s cliffhanger

Daniel’s “twenty-four hours” proposal sets up a psychological endurance match rather than a conventional disciplinary fight. The next chapter is expected to show whether he can force Mukhyn to confront the source of his behavior without resorting to the violence associated with other TRPA supervisors.
