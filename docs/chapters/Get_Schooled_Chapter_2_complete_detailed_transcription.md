**Source examined:** the uploaded MHT archive for Chapter 2. The archive contains the complete chapter: **14 reader pages assembled from 26 embedded image strips**.

**Content notes:** bullying, coerced alcohol consumption, physical assault, and repeated discussion of a student’s suicide.

### Transcription conventions

- **Panel numbering is editorial.** The original is a continuous-scroll webtoon, so some “panels” are borderless images or linked visual beats rather than traditional boxed panels.
- **Dialogue and captions are preserved as printed**, including grammatical oddities, unusual punctuation, capitalization, and scanlation line-break hyphenation when it materially reflects the source.
- **Visual** describes only what is shown. Interpretive observations are labeled separately.
- **SFX** records readable effects. Where a Korean effect has no single certain English equivalent, its visible function is described rather than given a falsely precise translation.
- Character names are used when the chapter identifies them. Where a speaker cannot be established with certainty, the transcript says so.

# Chapter-Level Character and Visual Notes

## Na Hwa-jin

Na moves through three deliberately contrasting modes in this chapter:

1. **Dry contempt in the principal’s office.** He does not rage at the attempted bribe. He humiliates the principal by treating the behavior as childish and embarrassing.
2. **Awkward substitute teacher.** In Class 2–5 he initially smiles, introduces himself politely, and begins attendance even while being ignored. This performance lets the students reveal their habitual behavior without first adapting to a visible threat.
3. **Field supervisor.** Once Hak-jae attacks, Na stops maintaining the ordinary teacher fiction. His physical control is immediate, but he still uses institutional language, the attendance folder, the class president, and a formal self-introduction. The violence is framed as part of a larger intervention rather than an uncontrolled outburst.

His most important investigative act is sensory and behavioral rather than technological: he notices that Byung-soo carries the smell of freshly consumed alcohol while Hak-jae smells hungover. That difference exposes the forced-drinking alibi.

## Kim Hak-jae

Hak-jae is the visible classroom enforcer. His defining behaviors are coercion, improvisational lying, and confidence that political protection extends to him through Jun-hyung. His authority proves shallow once directly challenged: after Na throws him, the class does not rally to defend him, and Jun-hyung later overrides the entire room without even lifting his head.

## Park Byung-soo

Byung-soo has almost no verbal agency in the chapter. His condition supplies evidence through the art: swelling, coughing, drool, sweat, fear, and the fresh alcohol odor. Hak-jae’s cover story depends on making those injuries appear self-inflicted. Na’s order to the class president is the first time an authority figure in the room acts to protect him rather than use him.

## Ryu Jun-hyung

Jun-hyung is introduced indirectly before he becomes active. He remains face-down through the forced drinking, the cleanup plan, Na’s entrance, Dae-seok’s name, Hak-jae’s attack, and Hak-jae’s defeat. This apparent passivity is not powerlessness. The instant he says “Sit,” the class reverses its response to Na. His authority is social and institutional: classmates fear him, Hak-jae relies on his political family connection, and the principal has already invoked lawmaker Ryu Kwang-pil while trying to deter scrutiny.

## Park Dae-seok’s absence

Dae-seok never appears alive in the chapter, but his absence organizes the attendance sequence. Na calls his name as though he is still a member of the class, revealing that:

- there is no preserved empty desk or visible memorial space;
- Hak-jae is comfortable degrading him after death;
- the principal’s explanation and Hak-jae’s explanation use closely related language about sexual frustration or pessimism;
- the class knows the name is dangerous but has normalized silence and mockery around it.

Na’s third “culture”—complacency after a classmate’s death—connects the classroom’s behavior to the administration’s concealment.
