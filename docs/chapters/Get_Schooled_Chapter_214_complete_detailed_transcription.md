## Strip 001 — Lee Jun-bin’s delinquent past

The youth-rights counselor is identified as **Lee Jun-bin**.

As a teenager, Jun-bin describes himself as cowardly rather than impressive.

He moved with other weak delinquents, extorted money from people less able to resist, and used tattoos and group intimidation to appear dangerous.

He avoided honest work and treated police stations and juvenile facilities like familiar places.

During one robbery or extortion attempt, an older blond man intervened.

The man handled Jun-bin with overwhelming ease.

That encounter became the beginning of Jun-bin’s change, though the full relationship is withheld for later chapters.

## Strip 002 — Adam’s House and Eve’s House

The documentary visits a Level 6 juvenile rehabilitation residence.

The boys’ residence is called:

> **ADAM’S HOUSE**

The girls’ residence is called:

> **EVE’S HOUSE**

The facilities are designed to rebuild a household structure rather than function only as locked punishment centers.

Jun-bin leads classes and counseling.

Residents write apology letters to their victims every day until they leave.

He tells them most victims may never answer.

Forgiveness cannot be demanded.

The letters are still part of learning to name what they did without excuses.

## Strip 003 — Rebuilding the missing family structure

Many residents come from homes shaped by:

- poverty,
- parental divorce,
- neglect,
- domestic violence,
- addiction,
- or long periods without supervision.

Their identities are blurred in the documentary.

One young person describes living through violence at home.

Another says constant phone use and running away became easier than returning to a household that did not feel safe.

A visible statistic says approximately **70 percent of juvenile offenders come from damaged or unstable homes**.

The chapter does not claim family hardship excuses crime.

It argues that punishment without changing the environment returns young people to the conditions that produced the behavior.

## Strip 004 — Kang Changsung prepares to leave

The residents hold a farewell celebration for **Kang Changsung**, the leader among the boys.

He promises to prove that he can live differently.

> **I WON’T COMMIT ANOTHER OFFENSE.**

The atmosphere is hopeful.

Then the TRPA team recognizes his name.

Before entering the residence the previous year, Changsung was connected to one of the agency’s most severe cases.

Three children disappeared.

They were eventually found locked inside a motel room, badly beaten and controlled through fear.

---

## Strip 005 — The old kidnapping and loan-shark case

The previous case is shown in flashback.

Changsung confined the missing children and used them in loan-related crime.

He demanded information connected to parents’ bank accounts, cards, or identities and used violence to force cooperation.

The children were kept under constant threat.

The TRPA subdued him after receiving a highly specific anonymous tip.

Even for the experienced inspectors, he was one of the most difficult youth offenders they had handled.

The documentary’s gentle, responsible resident leader is therefore not being contrasted with a minor mistake.

He committed serious, planned harm.

---

## Strip 006 — Changsung wants to ask victims for forgiveness

Changsung says he wrote apology letters every day.

None of the victims replied.

He now wants to meet them directly, kneel, and ask forgiveness.

Jun-bin warns that sincere remorse does not create a right to enter a victim’s life.

The victim may experience the meeting as another demand from the offender.

Changsung says he understands but believes facing them is the first step in taking responsibility.

Jun-bin answers that reflection is necessary—but must not become pressure disguised as apology.

---

## Strip 007 — Jun-bin’s public argument for rehabilitation

During the documentary interview, Jun-bin says juvenile crime partly reflects a failure of society.

The state should compensate victims rather than leaving every burden on individual families.

At the same time, the state should fund rehabilitation serious enough to prevent new victims.

Harsh punishment alone may brand a child permanently.

If every school, employer, and neighborhood treats the young offender as irredeemable, returning to crime can become the only identity left.

Jun-bin argues that rehabilitation is not softness toward offenders.

It is victim prevention.

---

## Strip 008 — One week later, Changsung is arrested again

Online commenters react furiously.

They accuse Jun-bin of caring more about offenders than victims.

Some say people like Changsung never change and that rehabilitation facilities are naïve shelters for criminals.

One week after the documentary, breaking news appears.

Eighteen-year-old Kang Changsung has been charged with assault.

The alleged victim is someone he previously assaulted.

According to the report, Changsung went to apologize, the victim rejected him, and Changsung attacked again.

The staff at Adam’s House are stunned.

Jun-bin’s face empties.

The final panel shows Changsung sitting alone in a detention cell, his expression unreadable and ominous.

# Chapter-level continuity notes

## The facility treats responsibility as a daily practice

Apology letters are not automatic reconciliation. They force offenders to revisit the harm while respecting the victim’s silence.

## Changsung’s old crimes were deliberate and severe

The rehabilitation question is intentionally tested through someone who cannot be dismissed as a harmless first offender.

## Jun-bin separates victim compensation from offender treatment

A functional system must support victims directly and also reduce the chance of future harm.

## Public criticism contains a real moral concern

Victims often receive less attention, funding, and patience than the offenders whose rehabilitation is publicly celebrated.

## The final arrest appears to validate every skeptic

The next chapters investigate whether Changsung truly relapsed or whether his past made him an easy person to frame.
