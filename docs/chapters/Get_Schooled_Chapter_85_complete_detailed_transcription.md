# Opening — Work phones become official policy

The principal gathers the teachers and announces a new Ministry of Education policy.

Every teacher will receive a work phone number. Teachers are no longer obligated to provide personal numbers or answer ordinary calls after 7 p.m.

After-hours complaints will go through the school’s integrated civil-service number. Genuine emergencies may still be connected to the teacher.

The principal expects parent opposition. A similar school policy was withdrawn the previous year after protests, but this version is a Ministry directive and takes effect immediately.

A notice is sent to families explaining that the system protects teachers’ right to rest and allows them to focus on education.

Parents react angrily in their group chat.

---

# Mrs. Choi recognizes Lim

Mrs. Choi meets Lim outside the classroom and recognizes her face and voice.

Lim explains that she has been teaching for only three days and already understands how exhausting constant parent contact can be.

She tells Choi that the work-number policy should allow her to rest.

Choi doubts it will last. The school attempted something similar before, but parent pressure forced it to withdraw.

---

# Parents confront the agency

A group of parents arrives to protest.

Lim formally identifies herself as a Teachers’ Rights Protection Agency inspector and says she used her authority to begin the work-number system because parent conduct violated teachers’ rights.

One mother screams that she works two jobs and can only call after normal hours.

She asks what she should do when her child refuses to discuss school and whether weekend counseling will simply make teachers complain about losing their days off.

Lim explains that after-hours calls are not completely prohibited:

- emergencies can be connected,
- appointments can be arranged,
- mutually agreed calls remain possible,
- and the school plans paid counseling leave so teachers can meet families without sacrificing personal rest.

---

# A reasonable exception

Another mother gives a more difficult example.

Her older son had asthma. His teacher used a separate work phone, but because the condition required close monitoring, the mother requested the personal number.

The teacher agreed.

Other parents then demanded the same treatment and accused the teacher of favoritism.

Lim acknowledges the real risk of discrimination controversies in a school serving more than a thousand students, parents, and staff.

Her answer is that exceptional decisions must be recognized as part of the teacher’s professional authority.

If the teacher must constantly “persuade” every other parent, no exception can be made safely.

When the school clearly protects the teacher’s authority to judge exceptional circumstances, the decision becomes professional discretion rather than personal favoritism.

---

# “You’re just a civil servant”

Some parents remain dissatisfied.

They say teachers are educators entrusted with precious children and should willingly endure inconvenience.

One says that drawing such a strict line shows a lack of affection.

Another dismisses teachers as **“just civil servants.”**

Lim asks who crossed the line first.

She says a small number of extreme parents have treated themselves as socially above teachers, creating the need for institutional boundaries.

The protesters shout that they are being called perpetrators and insist they are suffering too.

Lim warns them not to confuse public service with personal servitude.

Teachers and civil servants carry government authority while performing public duties.

A person who obstructs public work with threats, abusive language, false accusations, defamation, or exaggerated complaints may face criminal punishment.

The chapter ends on the parents’ shock at hearing that complaints can carry consequences for the complainant.

# Chapter-level observations

## 1. The policy does not eliminate communication

It moves communication into a system with hours, emergency routing, and professional judgment.

## 2. The asthma example shows the genuine complexity

Rigid equality can harm children with exceptional needs, but unprotected exceptions can expose teachers to accusations.

## 3. “Educator” is used to demand unpaid emotional labor

Parents invoke teaching as a vocation only when asking teachers to surrender privacy and rest.

## 4. The criminal warning targets method, not disagreement

Parents may raise concerns. The warning applies to obstruction, threats, defamation, and knowingly false complaints.
