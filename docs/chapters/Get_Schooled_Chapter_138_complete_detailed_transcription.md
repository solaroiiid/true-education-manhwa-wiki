> **KILL HIM!**

> **COULD IT BE… IS HE DEAD?**

Suchan looks down with a disturbing smile:

The children swarm the adult. They kick and beat him while shouting out the wrongs committed against them:

> **DIE! DIE! YOU BASTARD!**

## Reader Images 007–009 — Suchan orders a burial

Suchan rolls or drags the principal’s body. The other children hesitate.

> **BUT… YOU’RE REALLY GOING TO KILL…?**

Suchan screams back:

> **HAVE YOU ALL FORGOTTEN WHAT THIS BASTARD DID TO US?!**

He lists the school’s exploitation:

> **TAKING 1 MILLION WON FROM OUR PARENTS EVERY MONTH AND NOT EVEN PROVIDING PROPER MEALS!**

> **ALWAYS MAKING US WORK AND PITTING THE KIDS AGAINST EACH OTHER WITH BULLYING!**

A girl remembers what they suffered and hands the weapon back.

Suchan points toward the pond pit:

> **GUYS.**

> **BRING A SHOVEL. LET’S BURY THIS BASTARD.**

Some students obey, digging while Daniel watches. Suchan says that this is only returning what the principal made them endure.

One child is suddenly struck across the face by the handle of a shovel. The unexpected impact interrupts the mob’s momentum.

## Reader Images 010–012 — Daniel turns the logic of revenge on the children

Daniel stands between the group and the unconscious principal.

He asks:

> **WHY DID YOU DO SOMETHING UNNECESSARY AND PROVOKE THEM?**

He makes clear that his promise was escape, not revenge:

> **DID I SAY I’D HELP YOU LEAVE OR HELP YOU TAKE REVENGE?**

Suchan, bleeding from the head, insists that the principal deserves it. Daniel answers:

> **NO, DO YOU EVEN THINK YOU HAVE THE RIGHT TO SEEK REVENGE?**

Daniel explains that the principal is guilty, but the children have also harmed weaker students:

> **HUMANS ARE TRULY DECEITFUL.**

> **MOST REMEMBER THE EVIL DONE TO THEM BETTER THAN THE EVIL THEY’VE COMMITTED…**

> **BUT THAT WAY, THE CYCLE OF VIOLENCE PRODUCING VIOLENCE WILL CONTINUE.**

> **UNLESS SOMEONE STOPS IT.**

He says that if burial is the punishment for bullying, then the rule must apply consistently:

> **NOW, IT’S YOUR TURN TO BE BURIED.**

The students recoil.

> **WHAT…? WHY US…?**

Daniel looks across the group:

> **IS THERE EVEN ONE AMONG YOU…**

> **WHO DIDN’T BULLY THOSE KIDS?**

Three younger victims stand apart in silence. None of the older students can answer.

## Reader Images 013–015 — Public exposure creates competing victim and offender narratives

The case becomes national news. Broadcasts reveal the grotesque violence at the alternative school and identify victims who had lived under the class president, Lee Suchan.

The first reports focus on the institution’s abuse. Then some students disclose that Suchan himself committed violence against them. Other students point out that the whistleblower was also an aggressor. The public narrative becomes chaotic, dividing people into “victims” and “perpetrators” even though many children occupied both roles at different times.

Following the educational authority’s announcement, Suchan—the only offender old enough not to be treated as a very young juvenile—is arrested and brought to criminal trial.

The prosecution emphasizes the violence he committed and seeks an extremely severe sentence:

> **A HEAVY SENTENCE WAS INEVITABLE, DEMANDING IMPRISONMENT OF 5 TO 7 YEARS.**

Suchan stands in court in a detention uniform, head lowered.

## Reader Images 016–018 — The TRPA asks the court to consider adult abandonment

On the sentencing day, the court notes that the defendant admits the facts.

The Teachers’ Rights Protection Agency submits a written position regarding Suchan. The statement does not deny his offenses. It argues that the court must consider:

- Suchan was approximately fifteen.
- His parents effectively abandoned him.
- Older students assaulted him continuously.
- Adults encouraged or ordered the violence he later committed.
- There was no meaningful settlement with his victims.
- Most importantly, no responsible adult offered him a lawful route out of the institution.

The court partially accepts the agency’s reasoning. It cannot ignore the harm Suchan inflicted, but it also cannot impose the same punishment as though he were an autonomous adult who created the system.

The court orders that Lee Suchan be transferred to juvenile court rather than receiving the full adult criminal sentence demanded by the prosecution.

# Chapter-level continuity notes

## Daniel prevents revenge without denying the principal’s guilt

He never claims the principal is innocent or undeserving of punishment. His objection is that the children are reproducing the same rule—power determines who may hurt whom—and extending it to killing and concealment.

## Suchan’s moral position is deliberately complicated

Suchan rescued Minhye, suffered extreme abuse, organized an escape, bullied younger children, and nearly led a murder. The chapter rejects a clean binary in which he is only a victim or only an offender.

## The burial order reveals how quickly liberation becomes domination

The children begin by seeking freedom, but once the principal is down, Suchan orders others to dig a grave. His authority over the group immediately resembles the hierarchy he claims to be destroying.

## Daniel applies one rule to everyone

When the children justify violence as repayment, Daniel asks who among them never harmed someone weaker. His intervention is not a defense of the principal; it is a refusal to let victimhood become permanent authorization for further abuse.

## The court recognizes diminished responsibility without erasing harm

The TRPA opinion emphasizes abandonment, coercion, age, and adult complicity. Suchan is still processed through juvenile justice, and the lack of settlement with victims is explicitly acknowledged.

## Public criticism exposes the difficulty of mixed-status cases

Once media coverage labels Suchan an offender, any mitigating argument is portrayed as betrayal of victims. The chapter presents the agency’s role as preserving complexity when public debate demands a single villain.

## Daniel’s nonviolence is rooted in personal violence

The blood-soaked flashback suggests that his philosophy emerged after he participated in or witnessed lethal violence. The next chapter is positioned to explain why he believes force can stop an act without healing its cause.
