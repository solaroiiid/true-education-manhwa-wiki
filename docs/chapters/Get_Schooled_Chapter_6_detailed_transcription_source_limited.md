# Chapter-level observations

## 1. The fire is an experiential reversal

Jun-hyeong planned to turn gasoline and fire against the school. Na lets him confront the same danger under controlled conditions, then extinguishes it. The intact dialogue makes the instructional purpose explicit: Jun-hyeong’s actions reveal more than his words.

## 2. Fear is not treated as remorse

Jun-hyeong says “Save me” and “I’m sorry” only after the fire threatens him. Na focuses on what he actually did—running as soon as he recognized the slow-moving flame—and asks whether he now understands what he inflicted on others.

## 3. Responsibility begins after the danger ends

Na does not present physical terror as the complete punishment. His speech moves from experience to responsibility and then to Park Daesung’s irreversible death. The source gap unfortunately removes the end of his precise instruction.

## 4. The school case becomes a national argument

Na publicly exposes the evidence, the other bullies are taken into custody, and Ryu Gwang-pil’s political career collapses. At the same time, Na’s own methods become the subject of public outrage and confused online debate.

## 5. The narrator notices performative certainty

The glasses-wearing student observes that many critics do not know what occurred and merely pretend that they do. The chapter does not claim all criticism is invalid; it emphasizes the speed with which a complicated case becomes an identity-based public fight.

## 6. Ryu understands loyalty but not accountability

When he confronts Chey, Ryu speaks about party affiliation, shared history, and being discarded. He never begins by discussing Jun-hyeong’s victims. Chey answers by placing responsibility on the father who raised and protected the child.

## 7. Chapter 6 bridges directly into Chapter 7

The ending formally introduces Chey Kang-suk, his long political career, and his position as head of education. Chapter 7 then opens with the press conference for which his aide is preparing him here.
