A long white transition descends into a juvenile-prison changing room. Blue uniforms lie discarded across the wooden floor. Several teenage boys change back into street clothes. # Chapter-level observations

### 1. The juvenile-prison visit is a deterrent, not a redemption program

Na explicitly rejects the idea that a week of punishment can magically change someone’s nature. His method is to make repeat offending concrete: the same prison, the same inmates, and the same long-term future.

### 2. The chapter distinguishes institutional failure from moral innocence

The seven-time killer describes himself as a “monster” created after adults failed to intervene. The chapter does not excuse his murders; it shows how the absence of protection helped turn victimization into lethal retaliation.

### 3. Na’s authority has a hard limit

Na can scare juvenile offenders and investigate schools, but he cannot freely punish someone already sentenced. The final interview deliberately places Ga-yoon’s unrepentant killer across a table from him, where personal vengeance and legal duty conflict.

### 4. The cliffhanger is built through stillness

There is no attack in the final panels. The prisoner’s smile, clasped hands, and mocking recollection are enough. Na’s silence and darkened expression carry the threat into Chapter 35.
