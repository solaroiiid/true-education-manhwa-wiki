# Reader page 1 — The accelerated judgment

The administrative case has already reached a decision.

The youth-rights attorney remarks that the speed is unusual. Administrative trials commonly take several months, but this one moved from citation to judgment in roughly two weeks.

He attributes the acceleration to the intense public interest surrounding the Teachers’ Rights Protection Agency. Even without an explicit command, the agency’s prominence and the media attention around the case exert pressure on everyone involved.

Na Hwa-jin listens without looking troubled.

# Reader page 2 — Hong Song-hak is ordered to transfer

Hong Song-hak grips the written ruling.

The decisive order is clear: the perpetrator responsible for the school violence must transfer to another school.

Song-hak’s face contorts.

> “WHAT THE HELL IS THIS?!”

He had assumed the five-day suspension was the final consequence and that Sang-wook’s administrative request could be endured like the earlier special-education program.

The transfer removes the condition Sang-wook feared most: being forced to share the same school with the person who repeatedly retaliated against him.

# Reader page 3 — “Power won, not reasoning”

The opposing lawyer says:

> “POWER WON THIS OVER, NOT REASONING.”

He argues that the result does not prove Lim Han-rim’s case was legally or philosophically superior. The Teachers’ Rights Protection Agency’s visibility made officials unwilling to appear soft on a notorious bully.

Na denies that the agency directly influenced the committee.

The lawyer replies that direct pressure is not necessary. A highly public institution can alter a judgment merely by standing beside one party.

The exchange preserves the arc’s tension: the transfer protects Sang-wook, but the process may still have been affected by politics and public fear.

# Reader page 4 — Song-hak loses his martial-arts identity

After the hearing, Song-hak returns to the gym or martial-arts school where he trained.

His instructor confronts him and throws or kicks him out.

The coach says shame is not created by losing a fight. Shame comes when the strength and technique a teacher gave someone are used to torment weaker people.

He returns Song-hak’s fees and orders him never to come back.

Song-hak had treated his body and training as proof that he deserved power. The instructor now rejects him precisely because of how he used that power.

# Reader page 5 — The public recognizes him

Outside, strangers notice Song-hak.

They recognize his face from circulated photographs and online discussion.

> “ISN’T THAT HONG SONG-HAK?”

> “THE ONE WHO GOT TRANSFERRED AFTER THE TEACHERS’ AUTHORITY CASE?”

People whisper, point, and openly express disgust.

Song-hak runs.

The social exposure extends beyond the school. The reputation he once used to frighten classmates now follows him as public disgrace.

# Reader page 6 — Tag or karma

The youth lawyer questions whether a minor should carry a lifelong label for conduct committed during adolescence.

Na answers that the meaning of the record depends on what Song-hak does next.

If he refuses to reflect, the result will feel like a permanent tag imposed by others.

If he genuinely understands what he did, the consequences become karma—a fact he accepts and uses to change.

Na says the first step toward receiving another opportunity is sincere reflection. An “opportunity” granted before that step merely restores the perpetrator’s freedom to continue.

Yeri reassures her with exaggerated confidence, joking that she is even more useful than Lim Han-rim.

# Reader page 11 — The vulnerability of runaway children

Yeri explains what happens to children who run away without money or guardians.

They become hungry, sleep on benches or in stairwells, and cannot obtain ordinary work or housing.

That desperation makes them easy targets for adults who call themselves “helpers.”

These adults offer food, shelter, or money, but many expect control, sex, labor, or criminal participation in return.

Yoo-jung visibly fears that Yoon-jung may already be trapped by one of them.

---

# Reader page 12 — “Helpers”

The chapter presents images of social-media messages, anonymous adults, motels, cars, and vulnerable youths.

Yeri says that the word “helper” sounds compassionate, which is exactly why it is dangerous.

A runaway child may have no reason to trust police, parents, schools, or shelters. An adult who claims to understand can therefore become the only apparent option.

Na listens more seriously as the scale of the problem becomes clear.

---

# Reader page 13 — The new assignment

Yeri insists that this is not merely one missing-person case.

The system surrounding runaway children allows exploiters to operate openly while presenting themselves as rescuers.

Na agrees to begin looking for Yoon-jung.

The chapter closes on the possibility that the girl’s disappearance is connected to a wider network of adults profiting from runaway minors.

# Chapter-level observations

## 1. Sang-wook’s practical request is granted

The ruling does not satisfy every legal concern, but it separates him from the person who repeatedly retaliated after formal intervention.

## 2. Song-hak’s consequences spread beyond school

The transfer, loss of martial-arts training, and public recognition remove several identities that protected his sense of superiority.

## 3. The chapter refuses an easy victory

The opposing lawyer’s criticism remains meaningful. The agency’s public power may affect institutions even when no direct pressure is applied.

## 4. The next arc begins with a structural gap

Runaway children can be legally required to attend school while simultaneously having no safe home, job, or shelter. “Helpers” exploit that contradiction.
