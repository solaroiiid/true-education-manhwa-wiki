# Strips 005–08 — Why the case is being reconsidered

The hearing summarizes both positions.

The case requires reconsideration because Hong Song-hak retaliated even after the school-violence committee issued its disposition.

The claimant argues that the committee misjudged a perpetrator who did not reflect on his conduct.

The respondent side argues that the agency’s excessive special education harmed Song-hak’s mind and body.

A committee member asks Lim why the training was so severe.

Lim says she understands the committee’s emphasis on correction.

Juvenile judgment systems focus on correction rather than simple punishment.

The Teachers’ Rights Protection Agency, however, exists for students and faculty whose rights have already been violated.

Its immediate activity is therefore more focused on relieving and protecting victims.

In this case, the victim’s rights were violated again because weak punishment failed to discourage retaliation.

Lim says the agency used a limited five-hour special-education session to prevent continued retaliation.

> “I THINK IT WAS A NECESSARY MEASURE.”

# Strips 009–12 — Environmental causes and another chance

The lawyer asks whether Lim believes perpetrators should be punished immediately for the victim’s sake, without any room for correction.

Lim says that is not her position, but asks which side the system chooses to prioritize.

The lawyer calls her thinking disgracefully simple.

He explains why juvenile dispositions emphasize correction.

Many juvenile offenders grow up amid poverty, parental neglect, and domestic violence.

They may be forced into the street, gather with other children in similar circumstances, and commit delinquency.

Children cannot choose the environment into which they are born. The environment therefore bears part of the responsibility for the crime.

Because individual blame is harder to isolate, adults who created these conditions should offer an opportunity for return through correction and education.

# Strips 013–18 — Lim’s online-game analogy

Lim asks the lawyer whether he plays games.

Before becoming an agency supervisor, she did not know much about contemporary children. An older man advised her to play games and experience their culture.

Lim had little computer or gaming experience. Her teammates frequently insulted her and tried to surrender because her mistakes put them at a disadvantage.

She admits that, as a soldier, she could not bring herself to press the surrender button.

She then describes what happened in one-sided matches.

The winning team often refused to end the game. Instead, they prolonged the match and continued humiliating the losing side.

Lim calls this a mechanism of school violence.

Human beings can experience pleasure from feeling superior to an opponent. In school violence, that pleasure may become the purpose.

Because the pleasure is persistent, simply telling the aggressor to stop is like placing cake in front of someone who loves sweets and ordering them not to eat it.

Lim argues that the most important practical response is physical separation of the perpetrator from the victim.

# Strips 019–22 — Cause versus pleasure

Lim says she respects the juvenile law’s corrective purpose and the committee’s goals.

A distinction is still necessary:

- Was the conduct primarily caused by an abusive or deprived environment?
- Or was the conduct pursued for personal pleasure?

If every case is dismissed under a single general explanation, she asks, what is education for?

The lawyer demands a basis for claiming that Song-hak abused others for pleasure.

Sang-wook answers.

When Song-hak came to his home to retaliate, he said he did not care about the agency or the school-violence committee.

What he could not tolerate was seeing his “toys” stand above him.

The statement directly identifies domination and enjoyment as his motive.

# Chapter-level observations

## 1. Lim distinguishes explanation from universal excuse

She accepts that poverty, neglect, and abuse can help produce juvenile offending. She rejects applying that explanation automatically to a student who openly describes victims as toys.

## 2. Separation is framed as protection, not vengeance

Lim’s practical conclusion is not that every aggressor should be beaten. It is that the aggressor should not remain beside the victim while correction is attempted.

## 3. Sang-wook’s revenge thoughts do not define his formal request

He admits the thoughts rather than presenting himself as emotionally pure. His requested outcome remains the ability to attend school normally without Song-hak.

## 4. The cliffhanger questions institutional neutrality

The statement that the result was set from the beginning suggests that persuasive testimony may have mattered less than prior institutional interests or bias.
