## Strip 003 — The opposition emphasizes development

The opposing panel says extreme crimes committed by children under fourteen represent a very small portion of juvenile offending.

The TRPA’s work naturally exposes Na to the worst cases.

Those reports should not be mistaken for the average child.

The speakers cite incomplete brain development, limited impulse control, and the importance of rehabilitation.

Criminal punishment may satisfy public anger without improving the child’s future behavior.

Na agrees that children are immature.

That immaturity, he says, is exactly why a clear and direct boundary matters.

A system that communicates no meaningful consequence until an arbitrary birthday can itself encourage dangerous experimentation.

## Strip 004 — Punishment and rehabilitation are separated

A panelist asks whether criminalizing middle-school children will genuinely rehabilitate them.

Na answers that the public debate often confuses two questions:

- whether a child requires rehabilitation;
- and whether a serious act requires a recognized consequence.

The existence of developmental limits does not make victims’ injuries temporary.

The law needs a standard that communicates responsibility without pretending every young offender is an adult.

The disagreement is not whether children can change.

It is how much responsibility can be imposed while change is still possible.

## Strip 005 — Lee Jun-bin broadens the cause

Lee Jun-bin challenges both sides.

He says a sharp age threshold can become an excessive leap, and not every child placed above it will enter prison.

He has also spent years emphasizing the socioeconomic and domestic conditions behind juvenile crime.

An opponent asks whether his support for lowering the threshold contradicts that work.

Jun-bin says it does not.

The household is precisely why changing the child’s legal status alone is insufficient.

Many serious offenders return each night to adults who:

- neglect them;
- profit from crime;
- encourage denial;
- or abuse them until violence becomes normal.

## Strip 006 — The Parent Responsibility Act enters the debate

Jun-bin says Korea lacks a sufficiently strong parental-responsibility framework.

He refers to an overseas school-shooting case in which parents received prison sentences of approximately ten to fifteen years.

The chapter uses the example to argue that adults may be criminally responsible when their own conduct materially enables a child’s serious violence.

Na and Jun-bin introduce the Yunseong case.

The father was neglectful, violent, and financially exploitative.

Ordinary civil liability had no effect.

Only when consequences reached the father personally did he begin:

- answering police calls;
- appearing for his son;
- apologizing to victims;
- and attempting basic care.

Whether his heart transformed completely mattered less than the change in behavior.

## Strip 007 — A student asks about abusive parents

A student in the audience, identified approximately as **Lee Munwon**, asks a difficult question.

Would a parent facing criminal liability simply beat the child more severely?

The panel acknowledges the danger.

Jun-bin and Na answer that parental responsibility cannot mean unlimited private authority.

Abuse is itself evidence that the guardian has failed and should face intervention.

A parent cannot avoid responsibility by claiming the child was beaten “for discipline.”

The visible return to Yunseong’s home shows the child striking his father with the bat after the father’s late attempt at reconciliation.

The law must account for both directions of violence without pretending the history began at the final blow.

# Chapter-level continuity notes

## The debate resists a single-variable solution

Age, punishment, rehabilitation, family environment, and parental accountability are treated as interdependent.

## Developmental immaturity cuts both ways

It supports reduced culpability and also supports earlier, clearer intervention.

## Behavioral change can precede emotional change

The father begins acting differently because of pressure before he understands parenting internally.

## Parental responsibility requires protection from parents too

A law cannot be credible if abusive guardians are allowed to call violence “discipline.”

## The final image prevents policy abstraction

The argument concerns real people whose histories cannot be repaired by changing a number alone.
