# Get Schooled / The Great War Character Psychology Profile

> This is an interpretive personality, psychology, and intelligence profile based on the available *Get Schooled* transcription corpus through Chapter 255 and the supplied *The Great War* Chapters 25 and 52. It is not a clinical diagnosis.

# Choi Ga-yoon

> **Name note:** English scanlations variously render her name as **Choi Ga-yoon**, **Choi Gayoon**, and **Choi Gayeon**. This profile uses **Choi Ga-yoon**, while preserving other spellings when discussing quoted or chapter-specific material.

---

## Source basis and confidence

Ga-yoon is unusual among the characters in this profile collection because much of her characterization is **posthumous**.

In *Get Schooled*, she is mainly reconstructed through:

- Na Hwa-jin’s grief and actions;
- G.Y.’s possessive and distorted account of her;
- references to her work as a teacher;
- the criminal appropriation of her name;
- and the continuing consequences of her death.

The supplied *The Great War* chapters add more direct information.

### The Great War — Chapter 25

The supplied MHT contained **87 ordered story images**. The chapter explicitly establishes that Ga-yoon:

- was a trusted older girl from the neighborhood;
- tutored a younger student;
- was Hwa-jin’s childhood friend and fiancée;
- fulfilled her dream of becoming a teacher;
- sincerely believed people could change for the better;
- and continued trying to rehabilitate a dangerous person until that person killed her.

### The Great War — Chapter 52

The supplied MHT contained **34 ordered story images**. Ga-yoon appears directly and is described as having learned about a crisis, identified someone who could help Hwa-jin, and sent that person to him.

This shows her not only as compassionate, but as **socially informed, proactive, and capable of mobilizing people around a problem**.

### Interpretive limitation

Because she receives less sustained interior narration than Hwa-jin, Hanrim, Sera, or Daniel, several conclusions below are necessarily probabilistic.

The strongest conclusions are those repeated across both series:

- she was deeply committed to education;
- she believed in rehabilitation;
- she actively helped and connected people;
- she inspired durable trust and attachment;
- and she was vulnerable to persisting with a person whose violence exceeded what care or teaching could safely contain.

---

# Core conclusion

**Choi Ga-yoon is a principled relational idealist whose belief in human change is expressed through active responsibility rather than passive optimism.**

She does not merely say that people can improve.

She:

- tutors younger people;
- becomes a teacher;
- enters difficult relationships rather than dismissing someone as hopeless;
- notices crises outside her immediate responsibility;
- and connects people who may be able to help one another.

Her compassion is therefore **interventionist**.

She sees another person’s unrealized potential and feels personally drawn to create the conditions under which it might emerge.

> **Ga-yoon’s defining belief is that a person should not be permanently reduced to the worst version of themselves when someone still has the ability to reach them.**

That belief is morally powerful.

It is also the source of her greatest danger.

The available material suggests that Ga-yoon may have treated the possibility of another person’s change as a responsibility she herself was obligated to carry—even when the other person repeatedly refused, exploited, or endangered that effort.

A concise description would be:

> **Ga-yoon is a teacher by vocation and temperament: she sees what a person might become, then risks too much trying to help them reach it.**

---

# 1. Dominant personality structure

## Extremely high compassion

Ga-yoon’s compassion is not restricted to people who are already easy to love.

She appears willing to invest in:

- younger students;
- troubled individuals;
- socially discarded people;
- and people whose present behavior gives others little reason for hope.

That breadth matters.

Many characters care deeply for innocent victims. Ga-yoon’s distinctive trait is that she also believes a person causing harm may still contain a recoverable self.

This does not prove that she excuses wrongdoing.

It shows that she resists **identity foreclosure**: the idea that one period of violence, delinquency, or failure permanently defines the entire person.

## Very high conscientiousness

Becoming a teacher was not an accidental career.

*The Great War* describes it as a dream she finally achieved.

Her earlier tutoring also suggests that education was part of her identity before it became her profession.

Teaching requires:

- preparation;
- patience;
- repeated explanation;
- emotional persistence;
- and willingness to work toward outcomes that may not appear immediately.

Ga-yoon seems to carry this teaching orientation into her personal moral life as well.

## High social confidence

Ga-yoon does not appear socially passive.

In Chapter 52, she gathers enough information about a crisis to locate someone from Hwa-jin’s middle-school past and directs that person toward him.

That requires confidence in:

- approaching people;
- assessing their usefulness;
- sharing sensitive context;
- and trusting her own judgment about who should meet whom.

Her helping style is not limited to private emotional support.

She moves through social networks.

## Moderate dominance

Ga-yoon is not presented as physically or socially domineering in the manner of Hwa-jin or Hanrim.

Nevertheless, her actions suggest quiet authority.

People listen when she:

- tutors them;
- recommends a course of action;
- sends someone to assist;
- or commits herself to another person’s rehabilitation.

Her dominance is relational rather than coercive.

She influences through trust, moral clarity, and the expectation that people are capable of better behavior.

## High openness to human complexity

Ga-yoon appears unwilling to divide people neatly into good and evil categories.

Her belief in change implies tolerance for contradiction:

- a harmful person can retain human value;
- a damaged person can still possess agency;
- and someone’s present identity may not be their final one.

This is psychologically sophisticated when paired with boundaries.

Without boundaries, it becomes dangerous optimism.

# 2. Her moral psychology

## Rehabilitation as a moral obligation

Ga-yoon’s belief in change is stronger than abstract hope.

The wording in *The Great War* indicates that she **tried to rehabilitate** a dangerous individual rather than merely wishing that he might improve.

This suggests an ethic built around participation:

> **If change is possible and I have the ability to help, then I should try.**

That principle anticipates one of *Get Schooled*’s central debates:

- Hwa-jin emphasizes accountability and consequence.
- Lee Jun-bin emphasizes rehabilitation and reintegration.
- Ga-yoon’s legacy sits painfully between them.

She appears to have believed in rehabilitation before the later characters formalized it into law, counseling, or public policy.

## A person is more than their worst behavior

Ga-yoon’s worldview likely separates:

- the person;
- the harmful behavior;
- and the possible future self.

This is a core educational stance.

A teacher cannot meaningfully teach while assuming every mistake reveals permanent incapacity.

Her moral imagination therefore seems developmental:

> people are unfinished.

## Help is practical, not sentimental

Chapter 52 is especially important because it prevents Ga-yoon from being reduced to a soft-hearted martyr.

She gathers information, identifies a potentially useful contact, and directs him toward Hwa-jin.

That is practical problem-solving.

Her care takes forms such as:

- tutoring;
- professional teaching;
- referral;
- networking;
- and sustained personal intervention.

She does not merely feel sympathy.

She asks what resource, person, or relationship might change the outcome.

## Relational responsibility

Ga-yoon appears to experience relationships as carrying duties.

If she knows:

- a student is struggling;
- a person is moving toward violence;
- Hwa-jin is facing a crisis;
- or someone possesses knowledge that could help,

she is unlikely to view the matter as none of her business.

This produces extraordinary loyalty and usefulness.

It may also make disengagement feel morally equivalent to abandonment.

## Opposition to possessive power

The G.Y. material repeatedly frames Ga-yoon through the language of possession.

Criminal figures describe Hwa-jin as the man who “stole” the boss’s woman, and later hostility toward him is tied to the imprisoned boss’s grievance over her.

That language reveals the surrounding criminal worldview more than it reveals Ga-yoon.

She chose Hwa-jin.

Her autonomy contradicted a man who interpreted attachment as ownership.

Her relationship choice therefore had political meaning inside that violent hierarchy: she refused to let another person’s desire define her obligations.

# 3. Emotional architecture

## Warmth expressed through involvement

Ga-yoon’s affection appears active.

She tutors, checks on people, discovers hidden problems, sends help, and remains engaged with difficult individuals.

Her emotional language is therefore likely behavioral:

> “I care, so I will participate.”

## Hope as both value and coping structure

Believing people can change may have helped Ga-yoon tolerate disappointment.

A failed interaction need not mean the relationship is hopeless if growth remains possible.

This gives her persistence.

It can also delay recognition that a pattern has become dangerous rather than merely difficult.

## High tolerance for emotional labor

Teaching and rehabilitation require repeatedly encountering:

- resistance;
- defensiveness;
- shame;
- dishonesty;
- and regression.

Ga-yoon appears capable of remaining emotionally invested through these experiences.

The “bottomless cup” description, however hostile or grief-stricken its speaker may be, implies prolonged effort with little reciprocal change.

## Likely difficulty abandoning someone

The strongest psychological vulnerability supported by the source is not naïveté in a general sense.

It is **disengagement difficulty**.

Ga-yoon may have understood that the person she was helping was dangerous.

She may nevertheless have believed:

- one more conversation could matter;
- abandoning him would confirm his worst beliefs;
- a teacher should not give up;
- or leaving would make any later harm partly her responsibility.

That pattern resembles overresponsibility rather than simple ignorance.

## Emotional courage

Working with a person capable of serious violence requires courage.

Ga-yoon’s death should not retroactively make every prior decision foolish.

The chapters present her as someone who knowingly entered difficult moral territory because she believed another human being remained reachable.

The tragedy is that courage and compassion do not guarantee control over another person’s choices.

---

# 4. Teacher identity and self-concept

## Teaching was a vocation

Ga-yoon dreamed of becoming a teacher and achieved that goal.

This means education was likely tied to:

- purpose;
- identity;
- and moral self-respect.

She did not merely work at a school.

She appears to have understood herself as someone responsible for helping others develop.

## Tutoring preceded professional teaching

Her role as the “noona next door” who tutored a younger person indicates that teaching behavior emerged before occupational status.

This supports the idea that pedagogy was dispositional.

She naturally occupied the role of:

- explainer;
- guide;
- encourager;
- and older person who notices potential.

## Possible fusion of worth with usefulness

A cautious inference is that Ga-yoon may have derived substantial self-worth from being able to help.

When helping works, this creates meaning.

When it fails, the person may conclude not merely that the method failed, but that they personally failed the other human being.

Such identity fusion can make healthy withdrawal psychologically difficult.

## Teacher mentality applied beyond safe boundaries

Classroom teaching is embedded in institutional safeguards:

- colleagues;
- rules;
- schedules;
- documentation;
- and limits on contact.

Trying to rehabilitate a dangerous adult or criminal through personal relationship lacks those safeguards.

Ga-yoon may have carried a teacher’s persistence into a context where a teacher’s role alone was insufficient.

# 5. Relationship psychology

## Na Hwa-jin: childhood intimacy, trust, and moral influence

Ga-yoon was Hwa-jin’s childhood friend and fiancée.

The relationship therefore developed across multiple identities:

- neighbors or childhood companions;
- young people growing into adulthood;
- trusted friends;
- and eventual romantic partners.

She also appears to trust his competence.

In *The Great War* Chapter 52, she sends someone to Hwa-jin because she believes that person can help him.

This suggests she did not see Hwa-jin merely as someone she needed to protect emotionally.

She recognized him as a capable partner and a node within her own helping network.

### Her influence on Hwa-jin

Hwa-jin’s later personality cannot be reduced entirely to Ga-yoon’s death, but her legacy clearly shapes him.

She represents the rehabilitative ideal he both admires and fears.

His later tension around whether offenders can change is not abstract.

He has seen:

- a person he loved believe in change;
- that belief exploited;
- and her life taken.

This helps explain why Hwa-jin can show great care while remaining suspicious of unguarded optimism.

By Chapter 237, his promise to “take back Gayeon’s name” shows that his campaign against G.Y. is partly an attempt to restore her identity from the criminal meaning imposed upon it.

## The person she tried to rehabilitate

The supplied chapter refers to the person as a dangerous “scumbag” who ultimately killed her.

The exact chain of identity and culpability is tangled across scanlation wording and later criminal testimony, so this profile does not silently resolve every ambiguity.

What is psychologically clear is that Ga-yoon continued investing in someone who did not reciprocate her ethical view of relationship.

She treated him as a person capable of becoming better.

He appears to have treated her care as something he could possess, consume, or punish.

That asymmetry was fatal.

## Students and younger people

Ga-yoon’s tutoring and teaching dream indicate that she likely related to younger people through developmental faith.

She seems to ask:

- What are they capable of learning?
- What happened to produce this behavior?
- What support would make another path possible?

This does not mean she lacked standards.

Effective teaching normally requires expecting effort and change rather than merely comforting.

## Wider social network

Chapter 52 shows her operating as a connector.

She notices that one person’s history or knowledge may be useful to another and creates the meeting.

This is a form of **network intelligence**:

- identifying latent resources;
- recognizing complementary roles;
- and moving information between otherwise separate people.

# 7. Primary strengths

## Developmental vision

Ga-yoon sees people in time rather than as frozen identities.

She can imagine a future self that the present person cannot yet embody.

## Sustained compassion

Her care survives difficulty.

She does not reserve moral concern only for cooperative or socially approved people.

## Practical helpfulness

She tutors, teaches, gathers information, and makes introductions.

Her kindness has logistics.

## Loyalty

Her relationships appear durable and action-based.

She does not disappear when someone becomes inconvenient.

## Moral independence

Her choices resist the possessive hierarchy surrounding G.Y.

She chooses her own partner, work, and moral commitments.

## Capacity to inspire others

Her death does not erase her influence.

Hwa-jin’s work, grief, and later demand to reclaim her name all demonstrate that her values remained psychologically alive in the people who knew her.

# 8. Primary weaknesses and blind spots

## Overresponsibility

Ga-yoon may have believed that if she could imagine a path toward someone’s change, she was obligated to keep providing it.

That converts possibility into personal duty.

## Weak disengagement boundaries

A teacher must persist.

A private individual facing a violent person must also know when persistence has become exposure.

The source suggests Ga-yoon remained involved beyond the point at which the relationship was safe.

## Optimism about latent goodness

Seeing potential is not the same as accurately estimating motivation.

A person may be capable of change without currently wanting it.

Ga-yoon’s greatest error may have been treating capacity as readiness.

## Possible rescue-identity fusion

Helping seems central to who she is.

Walking away could therefore feel like betraying both the other person and her own identity as a teacher.

## Underestimating possessiveness

A person who interprets care as ownership may not experience compassion as an invitation to change.

He may experience it as proof that the helper belongs to him.

The G.Y. language surrounding her suggests this dynamic became central.

## Insufficient structural protection

Her rehabilitation effort appears personal rather than embedded in:

- professional treatment;
- legal supervision;
- a team;
- or enforceable safety boundaries.

Compassion was asked to perform work that required institutions and containment.

# 10. Ga-yoon’s place in the series’ larger psychology

## She embodies rehabilitation before it becomes policy

Lee Jun-bin later articulates a formal philosophy of rehabilitation:

- punishment alone does not create a lawful identity;
- people should not be permanently reduced to past crime;
- and change requires continued support.

Ga-yoon embodies a more personal, less institutionally protected version of the same faith.

Her death shows why rehabilitation requires more than one compassionate person.

It requires:

- boundaries;
- accountability;
- trained support;
- legal protection;
- and recognition that the offender must actively participate.

## She explains part of Hwa-jin’s severity

Hwa-jin is not simply the opposite of Ga-yoon.

He carries part of her ethic.

He repeatedly gives people another chance, protects reforming youths from permanent public branding, and seeks change even while using force.

What he rejects is **unprotected faith**.

His harshness can be read partly as an attempt to build the consequence and containment that Ga-yoon’s compassion lacked.

## Her name becomes a conflict over identity

G.Y.’s appropriation of her name is psychologically violent.

It transforms:

- a teacher;
- a helper;
- an autonomous woman;
- and a believer in human change

into a symbol for an organization built on coercion, debt, possession, and violence.

When Hwa-jin says he will take back her name, he is not merely avenging a death.

He is rejecting the criminal narrative that reduced her to someone’s property and made her memory serve the very worldview she opposed.

---

# Final psychological verdict

Choi Ga-yoon should not be reduced to **“the dead fiancée who motivated Hwa-jin.”**

She had an independent moral identity.

She was:

- a tutor;
- a teacher by vocation;
- a social connector;
- a proactive helper;
- and a person who believed that harmful human beings could become more than their present behavior.

Her belief was not childish.

It was morally serious and behaviorally demanding.

The fatal problem was not that she believed change was possible.

The problem was that she appears to have treated another person’s possibility of change as a burden she personally had to carry, even when that person’s violence, possessiveness, and refusal made the task unsafe.

Her greatest strength and greatest vulnerability were therefore the same:

> **Ga-yoon refused to give up on people after the world had decided what they were.**

That capacity made her an extraordinary teacher.

Without sufficient boundaries, it also placed her life in the hands of someone who interpreted care not as a reason to change, but as something he was entitled to own.

The most accurate final description is:

> **Ga-yoon was not naïve because she saw goodness where none existed. She was tragically overcommitted because she saw human possibility and believed love, teaching, and persistence made her responsible for bringing it to life.**
