# Get Schooled Character Psychology Profile

> This is an interpretive personality, psychology, and intelligence profile based on the available transcription corpus through Chapter 255. It is not a clinical diagnosis.

# Han Yeri

> **Name note:** The scanlation sometimes presents her name in Western order as **Yeri Han**. This profile uses **Han Yeri**.

## Source basis

Yeri’s profile is primarily grounded in the Soyeon Girls’ High arc across Chapters 15–21, including her harassment of teachers, manipulation of classmates and adults, conflict with Lim Hanrim, ninth-grade trauma involving Sangyeol Cheon, and juvenile detention outcome.

---

## Core conclusion

**Han Yeri is a trauma-shaped social predator who protects herself from vulnerability by controlling every narrative before anyone else can define her.**

She is not unintelligent, impulsive in a simple sense, or merely “a bad student.” Yeri is highly observant, socially strategic, academically capable, and unusually skilled at recognizing which institutional rule will make an adult hesitate.

Her central psychological adaptation is to turn vulnerability into a weapon.

When she feels exposed, frightened, betrayed, or cornered, she does not ask for protection. She rapidly creates a story in which she is the endangered person and the other party becomes the aggressor.

> **Yeri’s deepest pattern is not simply lying. It is making sure that no one else gets to decide what happened to her ever again.**

That pattern developed after a real adult betrayal. It later became a method through which she harmed teachers, students, followers, and people trying to save her.

---

## 1. Dominant personality structure

### Extremely high social dominance

Yeri does not rely primarily on physical strength.

She dominates through:

- reputation;
- group coordination;
- accusations;
- selective vulnerability;
- sexualized embarrassment;
- and control over what witnesses believe they saw.

She understands that an adult teacher may be physically stronger while still being institutionally powerless.

This is why she can shave a teacher’s hair, provoke him into grabbing her wrist, and instantly transform the scene into:

> an adult man touching a frightened female student.

Her power comes from controlling interpretation.

### High extraversion and social boldness

Before her ninth-grade trauma, Yeri is described as attractive, outgoing, intelligent, popular, and academically ambitious.

Afterward, those same social capacities become predatory tools.

She approaches strangers confidently, asks invasive questions without shame, commands group attention, and performs emotions under pressure.

She rarely appears socially intimidated.

### High antagonism

Yeri reacts to boundaries as personal attacks.

A teacher who refuses humiliation, a follower who blocks her escape, or an adult who challenges her version of events quickly becomes an enemy.

Her hostility is not limited to people who originally harmed her.

Once obstructed, even loyal followers become disposable.

### Low visible anxiety, high underlying threat sensitivity

Yeri presents herself as fearless.

Yet her behavior suggests constant monitoring for:

- betrayal;
- loss of control;
- humiliation;
- exposure;
- and adult authority gaining the upper hand.

She appears calm because she attacks the threat before others can see that she feels threatened.

---

## 2. Trauma and defensive organization

### A real betrayal distorted her model of authority

In ninth grade, Yeri experienced severe abuse or violation by teacher Sangyeol Cheon.

The available transcription does not support reducing the event to a minor misunderstanding. It is associated with fear, coercion, and a profound destruction of trust.

Afterward, Yeri begins treating teachers as one hostile category.

Her implicit rule becomes:

> **An adult who appears caring is either lying now or preparing to betray me later.**

This explains why ordinary kindness does not easily reassure her.

### Identification with the aggressor

Yeri survives adult powerlessness by learning to reproduce the aggressor’s position.

Instead of remaining the student who can be cornered, disbelieved, or used, she becomes the person who:

- determines what others are allowed to say;
- makes people fear accusation;
- invades boundaries;
- and punishes anyone who resists.

She does not heal the original power imbalance.

She reverses it.

### Vulnerability becomes intolerable

Yeri can perform fear brilliantly, but genuine helplessness appears unbearable to her.

When she is physically or socially cornered, she escalates rather than submit.

The rooftop strategy is the clearest example. She attempts to create a situation in which Hanrim’s rescue itself can be framed as attempted violence.

Even being saved must remain under Yeri’s narrative control.

### Trauma explains the transformation without excusing it

The series explicitly preserves both facts:

- Yeri was seriously harmed by an adult.
- Yeri seriously harmed many other people.

Her victimhood is real.

So is her responsibility.

## 3. Moral psychology

### Morality organized around loyalty rather than universal rules

Yeri does not apply one stable principle to everyone.

Behavior is judged according to whether a person is:

- loyal to her;
- useful to her;
- humiliating her;
- or threatening her control.

A follower may participate in abuse without consequence until that follower interferes with Yeri’s escape. At that moment, the follower becomes punishable.

### Protection language as an instrument

Yeri is extremely skilled at using legitimate protective norms dishonestly.

She understands that schools must take seriously:

- physical contact between male teachers and female students;
- allegations of fear;
- student vulnerability;
- and adult misconduct.

The rights themselves are not false.

Yeri weaponizes the institution’s fear of violating them.

### Responsibility displacement

When consequences approach, Yeri repeatedly relocates blame:

- the teacher touched her;
- the inspector is chasing her;
- the follower stood in her way;
- Sunyoung betrayed her;
- police should protect her from the “crazy woman.”

Her internal moral logic focuses on what others did immediately before her retaliation, not on the larger chain she created.

### Retaliation as self-justification

Yeri experiences punishment of “betrayal” as inherently justified.

This allows an escalating cycle:

1. She harms someone.
2. The person resists or reports her.
3. Resistance becomes betrayal.
4. Betrayal authorizes more severe harm.

The system protects her self-image from admitting that the original aggression was hers.

## 4. Emotional architecture

### Humiliation sensitivity

Yeri appears especially reactive when another person makes her look:

- powerless;
- foolish;
- abandoned;
- exposed;
- or less influential than she believed.

She can tolerate physical pain more easily than public loss of control.

### Betrayal sensitivity

Sunyoung’s attempt to stop her is interpreted not as care but betrayal.

A follower who blocks her is attacked.

Hanrim’s intervention is experienced as an assault on Yeri’s autonomy even when it prevents her death.

This suggests that Yeri expects relationships to become coercive the moment another person stops obeying her.

### Anger protects grief and fear

Yeri’s accessible emotions are mockery, rage, amusement, contempt, and theatrical fear.

Grief, shame, longing, and terror are buried beneath action.

She does not appear to have integrated the fact that she was once genuinely powerless.

Instead, she organizes her personality around never occupying that position again.

### Emotional expression is partly theatrical

Yeri can move rapidly between:

- cheerful friendliness;
- sexualized teasing;
- crying;
- panic;
- rage;
- and cold amusement.

Some of those emotions may be genuine.

The speed and situational usefulness of the transitions show that expression is also strategic.

She treats emotion as evidence other people must react to.

## 5. Identity and self-concept

### Former high-achiever identity

Before the traumatic incident, Yeri was academically capable enough to consider a foreign-language high school.

Her later behavior should not be interpreted as low intelligence.

The tragedy is that a student with strong academic and social potential reorganized those abilities around domination and survival.

### “Psychopath” as defensive identity

Yeri calls herself a psychopath during the confrontation with Hanrim.

That statement is not a clinical diagnosis.

Psychologically, the label may serve several functions:

- frightening other people;
- rejecting pity;
- claiming that change is impossible;
- and turning damage into a powerful identity.

If she is inherently monstrous, she never has to admit that she was hurt, changed, and still has choices.

### Control as proof of safety

Yeri feels safest when:

- classmates obey;
- teachers hesitate;
- adults fear accusations;
- and witnesses accept her version first.

Her identity therefore depends on environments where she can control perception.

## 6. Relationship psychology

### Sangyeol Cheon: origin of generalized distrust

Cheon’s abuse becomes the template through which Yeri interprets teachers.

The danger is generalization.

Instead of distinguishing one abusive adult from all later adults, Yeri treats every teacher as a possible version of him.

### Sunyoung Jung: care interpreted as betrayal

Sunyoung initially survives by accommodating the class and later attempts to intervene more honestly.

Yeri cannot accept a caring adult who also imposes limits.

To her, love without obedience is hypocrisy.

Sunyoung’s testimony therefore feels like the ultimate betrayal: the person who understood her past still refuses to excuse her present.

### Lim Hanrim: uncontrollable witness

Hanrim is uniquely threatening because she resists all of Yeri’s usual tools.

She is:

- physically difficult to intimidate;
- socially unembarrassed;
- institutionally authorized;
- and willing to act despite reputational risk.

Yeri repeatedly tries to convert Hanrim from rescuer into perpetrator.

Hanrim’s refusal to abandon her—even during a fall—also confronts Yeri with a kind of adult protection that does not demand submission or permit abuse.

### Followers: audience and extensions of self

Yeri’s classmates help create her authority.

They laugh, witness, repeat narratives, and share risk.

However, their safety is conditional.

Once a follower stops functioning as an extension of Yeri’s will, the relationship loses value.

## 8. Primary weaknesses and blind spots

### Narrative overconfidence

Yeri becomes accustomed to controlling what people believe.

When investigators compare evidence across incidents, her separate stories begin forming a visible pattern.

### Escalation under loss of control

The closer she comes to exposure, the more dangerous and less strategically disciplined she becomes.

Her tactics shift from social manipulation toward cutters, bricks, home invasion, and rooftop risk.

### Inability to distinguish limits from betrayal

A person can care about her and still refuse to obey her.

Yeri interprets that distinction as hypocrisy.

This destroys potentially corrective relationships.

### Followers become disposable

Her social system depends on loyalty but does not offer reciprocal protection.

That eventually gives witnesses reasons to cooperate against her.

### Identity foreclosure

Calling herself irreparably dangerous protects her from the harder possibility that she remains responsible for choosing what she becomes next.

### Low capacity for remorse in the observed material

The corpus shows anger at consequences and betrayal more clearly than guilt toward victims.

That does not prove she can never develop remorse.

It means the available chapters do not show it becoming stable.

## Final psychological verdict

Han Yeri is one of the series’ clearest examples of a victim becoming an offender without ceasing to have been a victim.

Her intelligence is real.

Her trauma is real.

Her cruelty is also real.

She learned that institutions can ignore an abused child, so she later learned to make institutions fear her.

Her greatest strength is her ability to read social power in real time.

Her greatest danger is that she interprets every boundary as a reenactment of the original betrayal.

> **Yeri does not merely lie to escape consequences. She constructs realities in which she can never again be the powerless child—and destroys anyone who refuses to live inside them.**
